When a friend asks "Let's meet on Saturday?", most of us say yes and then go check. Now Nudge removes that check. It reads the message, knows what you've done, anticipates what you'll type next, and offers it as one chip above the keyboard. Users are forced to orchestrate

Someone texts, "Where's the restaurant we went last Friday?" To answer, you switch apps, search your history, copy the name, come back, and retype it. Every single time, for something this simple.

What people expect from AI

I ran a survey of 300 people and 8 in-person interviews. People wanted three things from AI, and those three became the lens for every decision that followed.

Knowing

It remembers who you are and what you've done.

Anticipating

It predicts what you need before you ask.

Acting

It does the steps for you.

"I know what needs to happen. I just hate doing all the steps to get there."Interview participant

Start where AI can act with confidence

In the survey, 82% felt most inefficient in productivity and 65% in communication. I started with communication anyway. In a conversation the context is already on screen and the action is right there, so AI can act with confidence.

Two kinds of nudge

Recall nudge: makes input easier

"Where's the restaurant we went last Friday?" Now Nudge picks out "restaurant" and "last Friday," checks your location history, and finds Pacific Catch, Friday at 7pm. That's what you were about to type, so it's waiting as a chip, ready to send.

Action nudge: see it before you tap

"Can you share photos of the trip to Seoul?" It finds photos tagged Seoul and offers one button, Share photos. Tap it and the gallery opens already filtered.

Where should a guess live?

At the top it looked like a notification. In the middle of the chat it blocked the conversation. By the navigation bar it interrupted whatever you were doing.

I landed on the keyboard. The keyboard isn't just input. It's the moment of intent. It never covers content, leads naturally into typing for recall nudges, and works in third-party chat apps like KakaoTalk and WhatsApp.

One nudge or many?

My product owner and I disagreed on how, not what. I designed one nudge at a time. He wanted a scrollable row: we can't always know what the user wants, so why limit them?

I pointed to Hick's Law, which says more options mean slower decisions, and a conversation isn't a browsing moment. Instead of arguing, we tested both, measuring time to choose and how many people scrolled.

1.4xslower to choose with the scroll version
2%ever scrolled to a second option

We shipped the single most confident suggestion.

Designed for the Fold

On the Fold, View calendar opens in split screen, so you check your schedule without leaving the conversation you're replying to.
